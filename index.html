The Bitter Lesson

“The biggest lesson that can be read from 70 years of AI research is that general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin.”

That is the opening line of Rich Sutton's short essay. It is easy to agree with. This page tries to make you feel it. Every result below is produced while you read, by the device you are reading on: games are played, networks are trained, and mistakes are counted. Nothing is pre-recorded.

Why would computation win? Sutton gives one reason: its price keeps collapsing. In 1961 a billion floating-point operations per second would have cost about $200 billion in today's money. In 2025 it cost about a cent. Over any stretch longer than a research project, far more computation shows up than anyone planned for. A method that turns extra computation into better results rides that curve. A method built from what its authors know does not, because knowledge doesn't get cheaper every year.

Sutton noticed the same four-step story repeating through AI's history:

  1. AI researchers have often tried to build knowledge into their agents,
  2. this always helps in the short term, and is personally satisfying to the researcher, but
  3. in the long run it plateaus and even inhibits further progress, and
  4. breakthrough progress eventually arrives by an opposing approach based on scaling computation by search and learning.

What follows are three small re-enactments of that history, each run from scratch on your device. In each one a program built from human knowledge meets a general method that knows almost nothing but can put more computation to use.

I · Search

The Expert and the Searcher

In May 1997 IBM's Deep Blue beat the world chess champion, Garry Kasparov. It still carried about 8,000 hand-tuned features of chess knowledge, but what won was search: on average it examined some 126 million positions a second. Many researchers had hoped that an understanding of how people play would win instead. John McCarthy called the result a measure of “our limited understanding of the principles of artificial intelligence.” Go repeated the story twenty years later. Then AlphaZero learned chess, shogi and Go starting from nothing but the rules.

Our chessboard is Connect Four: small enough to run on a phone, deep enough that whole books have been written about it. Meet the two players.

Knowledge

The Expert

Built from human Connect Four strategy. Win if you can; block if you must. Never play directly under your opponent's winning square. Make two threats at once. Prefer the centre, where more lines run. If you move first, aim for threats on odd rows; if second, on even rows (Victor Allis's odd/even rule, from the 1988 thesis that solved the game). Looks two moves ahead.

11 hand-tuned weights2-move lookaheadsame effort every move
Computation

The Searcher

Knows the rules and nothing else. Before each move it plays out imaginary games at random from the current position, thousands of them, and steers toward the moves that win more of them. This is Monte Carlo tree search, introduced in 2006 by Rémi Coulom and by Levente Kocsis and Csaba Szepesvári. Give it more imagined games and it plays better.

At each level of the tournament the Searcher gets four times as many imagined games per move as before and plays 32 games against the Expert, half of them moving first. Openings are randomised, two moves deep, and each opening is played once from each side. The Expert never changes. It can't: there is no dial on knowledge.

    
Look at what the Searcher never learns. Nobody tells it the centre matters, or that threats on odd rows are worth more, or that you shouldn't play under your opponent's threat. It works out what it needs, again, before every single move, by sheer volume of imagination. That is exactly what disappointed the chess researchers of 1997. In Sutton's telling, “they said that ‘brute force’ search may have won this time, but it was not a general strategy, and anyway it was not how people played chess.”

      II · Learning
      
Three ways to read a digit

    

    
Search is one way to spend computation. Learning is the other.

    
Speech recognition went through the lesson in the 1970s. In DARPA's speech-understanding program, Carnegie Mellon's Harpy, which compiled every sentence it could hear into one big network and searched it, met the program's goals. Hearsay-II, a “blackboard” on which separate experts in acoustics, phonetics, syntax and meaning pooled their knowledge, did not. Statistical models such as hidden Markov models took over the field in the following decade. Fred Jelinek, who ran IBM's speech group, recalled a remark of his from 1988: “Whenever I fire a linguist our system performance improves.”

    
Vision followed. For decades researchers designed edge detectors and features by hand, with names like SIFT and HOG. In 2012 a learned network, AlexNet, won the ImageNet challenge with a 15.3% top-5 error rate; the runner-up, built on hand-designed features, had 26.2%. “Today all this is discarded,” Sutton wrote.

    
Here are three programs that read handwritten digits, one from each era.

  

  

    

      

        Knowledge
        
The Rulebook

        
Rules written by hand, the way you might explain digits to a child: an 8 is two loops stacked up; a 6 has one loop, low down, and a stroke rising from it. It thins each digit to a skeleton, finds the loops, stroke ends and junctions, and applies its rules. It never trains.

        
hand-written rules0 FLOP of training

      

      

        Knowledge + learning
        
The Feature Engineer

        
Human-designed features with a learned decision on top. Each image is summarised as histograms of edge directions in small cells, the HOG features of Dalal and Triggs (2005), a design distilled from decades of vision research. A linear classifier learns which histograms mean which digit.

        
1,296 designed features12,970 learned weights

      

      

        Computation
        
The Learner

        
A small convolutional network. It starts as about 6,000 random numbers and is told only that nearby pixels belong together and that a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears. Everything else, including what an edge is, it has to learn from examples, one gradient step at a time.

The Rulebook was written for this page by an AI model, working the way researchers did in the 1970s: look at the digits it gets wrong, write a better rule, test again. Being a machine, it worked fast, and it logged every revision. Here is the diary. Hover over or tap a point to read what changed.

    
    
Each new rule fixed a dozen digits and broke a few others, and the curve went flat. Speed wasn't the problem: the AI wrote and tested a new rule every minute or so. The problem is the method. Every rule is a guess about how people recognise digits, and handwriting is more varied than any list of guesses.

      III · Generality
      
Change the rules

    

    
Sutton's second point is the deeper one:

    
“The actual contents of minds are tremendously, irredeemably complex … They are not what should be built in, as their complexity is endless; instead we should build in only the meta-methods that can find and capture this arbitrary complexity.”
Rich Sutton, The Bitter Lesson

    
Knowledge is always knowledge of a particular world. The Searcher's method is about any world that has rules. So let's change the world. Below, the Expert and the Searcher meet again, first on the Expert's home ground and then under four rule changes. The Searcher gets the same budget everywhere, 1,024 imagined games per move, and its code doesn't change. The Expert's code still runs, but its knowledge was written for one game.

Not every change breaks the Expert. Some of its knowledge is general: counting your open lines is a decent idea in any game of this family, and on the bigger board it can hold off a Searcher limited to 1,024 imagined games per move. When that happens, the Searcher's fix is the one it always has: more computation. The Expert's fix is a person, checking every rule and rewriting the ones that no longer hold. In misère, where four in a row loses, every piece of its wisdom points the wrong way. Without gravity it can't even move, because its code assumes pieces fall.

    
Invent a game

    
Make up your own rules. The Searcher has never seen your game and has no strategy for it, yet it will play it well from its first move, because it works out strategy from the rules at the moment it needs it.

Not everyone bet on computation. In 1984 Doug Lenat began Cyc, a project to write down common sense by hand, one assertion at a time. Its 1985 plan estimated about 150 person-years. In 2023 Lenat and Gary Marcus put the effort at four decades, roughly 2,000 person-years and tens of millions of hand-authored assertions. Today the language models at the top right of this chart pick up much of that common sense from text instead.

    
Sutton doesn't think language models settle the matter either. They “are clearly a way of using massive computation,” he said in 2025. “But they're also a way of putting in lots of human knowledge”: everything people have written. He expects systems that learn from their own experience to overtake them. The lesson is still being learned.

“We want AI agents that can discover like we can, not which contain what we have discovered. Building in our discoveries only makes it harder to see how the discovering process can be done.”

    
Rich Sutton, The Bitter Lesson, March 13, 2019

    
The bitterness is in whose knowledge loses. The Expert's rules, the Rulebook's loops and the Feature Engineer's edges are good human insights, carefully written down, and they really do help at first. Then the clock runs, the computation arrives, and a method that knows less wins. Sutton calls it a success “tinged with bitterness, and often incompletely digested, because it is success over a favored, human-centric approach.”

    
This page was built by an AI model, which is itself a product of the lesson: what it knows was learned from data with enormous computation, not written down by hand. It still wrote the Expert and the Rulebook by hand, to give computation a fair opponent, and then watched them lose.

  


  

    
How the experiments work

    
Tournament. Connect Four, 7 columns by 6 rows. The Searcher is plain UCT Monte Carlo tree search with uniformly random playouts, an exploration constant of 0.5, and proofs of forced wins and losses propagated inside the tree (MCTS-Solver), which also uses nothing but the rules. Levels run from 1 to 65,536 imagined games per move in steps of four; slower devices stop at 16,384. Each level is 32 games over 16 random two-move openings, each opening played once with each side moving first. A win scores 1, a draw ½. Whiskers are 95% intervals. Results vary a little from run to run because the openings and the Searcher's imagination are random.

    
Training race. 12,000 training images sampled at random (seed 1957) from MNIST's 60,000, and all 10,000 MNIST test images. Points along the way are measured on a fixed random 2,500 of the test images; each program's final point uses all 10,000. The Learner is a convolutional network (5×5 convolutions with 8 and then 16 filters, ReLU, 2×2 max-pooling, a linear output layer) trained with Adam at batch size 32, learning rate 0.003 with cosine decay, for 8 epochs, with its minibatch gradients computed in parallel across your device's cores. The Feature Engineer uses HOG features (4×4-pixel cells, 9 orientation bins, 2×2-cell blocks with L2-Hys normalisation, 1,296 numbers per image) and multinomial logistic regression trained the same way for 12 epochs. FLOP counts are analytic: a multiply-add counts as two operations, and the forward pass, backward pass and feature extraction are all counted. Evaluation is not. Neither program sees shifted or distorted copies of the data.

    
Rulebook. Written by hand for this page, with no fitted parameters: thresholds were set by looking at development digits, which were drawn only from the training set.

    
New worlds. Each variant is a 24-game match at 1,024 imagined games per move, over random two-move openings with sides swapped. If the Searcher scores under 50%, it plays another 24 games with four times the imagination, and again if needed, up to sixteen times.

    
Compute counts. “Positions imagined” counts every piece placed in an imagined game, plus the Expert's moves. “FLOP spent learning” counts training operations in Experiment 3.
